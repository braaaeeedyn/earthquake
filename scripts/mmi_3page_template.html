Estimated shaking intensity (MMI): answers to your questions

SeismicSoCal · 23 October 2026 · Braedyn Thompson

Data validation

How the two models were trained

  • Detect (is there a quake in this window?) was trained on labelled 30 s single-station windows, not on continuous data. Quake windows: 34,377 vertical-component windows with a picked P arrival, from 8,238 quakes (M 2.0–7.2), cropped so P falls anywhere 1–25 s in. Quake-free windows: 14,304 from 900 random times with no catalogued quake nearby, plus 2,062 from the old live system’s false alarms. Each batch is half quake, half quake-free.
  • Mixed, continuous data is tested separately: 80 held-out days of archive data replayed through the full live pipeline sent 145 pushes, all for real quakes. Confirmed-event precision was 86 % on busy days and 79 % on random days (chance 4 % and 0 %), and 68 % of M 3+ quakes were caught.
  • Size (magnitude), in plain terms:
    • Which quakes: all M 3+ quakes in the region from 2000 to Aug 2026 with at least 3 of our stations within 200 km (thinned so one aftershock swarm cannot dominate), plus a random 4,000 M 2–3 quakes, using only stations within 100 km: 6,243 quakes, a median of 5 stations each.
    • What it sees: for each quake, 30 s of shaking at every station that recorded it, starting just before the P wave, given as each recording’s shape, how big it was, and how far the station is from the quake.
    • What it does: it reads each station’s recording, lets nearby stations compare notes, and combines them into one magnitude.
    • How it learns: it guesses each training quake’s magnitude, is told the catalogue answer, and adjusts to make the miss smaller, over 40 passes through the 4,370 training quakes. Five copies are trained and averaged.
    • Practice for live conditions: in training the location is moved by a few km, the timing nudged by up to half a second, and some stations left out, because live sizing uses our own location and whichever stations report first.
    • How well: on 937 later quakes it never saw, it is off by 0.10 magnitude units on average (R2 0.951), against 0.886 for a simple amplitude-and-distance formula; with realistic errors and only 3–6 stations, R2 is 0.939. Large quakes are barely tested (test quakes: M 2–5.2).
  • Split: both models are split 70/15/15 by time, so a quake is never split between sets, and no quake used to train one model is in the other’s test set.

Detect’s test score, and what MCC means

  • Score: on the 7,303 test windows, MCC 0.886 and AUC 0.9998 (classic STA/LTA: AUC 0.816). MCC, the Matthews correlation coefficient, rates yes/no decisions using all four outcomes: quake windows flagged (TP), missed (FN), quiet windows flagged (FP) and passed (TN).
  • Calculation, on the 7,303 test windows at the threshold chosen on validation (0.9987):
MCC = (TP·TN − FP·FN) / √((TP+FP)(TP+FN)(TN+FP)(TN+FN)) = (4,562·2,339 − 0·402) / √(4,562·4,964·2,339·2,741) = 0.886

  • What the numbers mean: MCC runs from −1 (every window wrong) through 0 (no better than chance) to +1 (every window right). At 0.886, no quiet window was flagged and 402 of 4,964 quake windows (8 %) were missed, so 94.5 % of windows were right. Accuracy alone would mislead: answering “quake” every time scores 68 %, because the test set has about twice as many quake windows, yet its MCC is 0.
  • AUC 0.9998 does not depend on a threshold: a randomly chosen quake window scores above a randomly chosen quiet one 99.98 % of the time. Live, the trigger is lower (0.6): a few false triggers (1.4 % of quiet test windows) in exchange for fewer misses.

The data behind the shaking estimate

  • Ground-motion equation: log10 PGV = a + bM + c log10R + dR + e log10(VS30/631), a least-squares fit on the Size model’s 4,370 training quakes (25,526 records); held-out scatter 0.33 log10 (0.27 after the event correction).
  • Magnitude vs distance: most records are M 3–4 at 50–200 km. Only 7 training records are within 5 km and only 29 quakes are M 5 or larger, so close, large quakes are outside the calibrated range.
  • DYFI check: 57 quakes of M 4.05–5.22 (2022–2026), all after the training period. The 29 older ones (2,088 cells) were used to fit the offset; the 28 newer ones (1,892 cells) only for scoring. Reference: USGS “Did You Feel It?” intensity (CDI) in 10 km cells with 3+ responses.
  • 0.42 is the mean absolute error over cells, not the signed error. The bias is +0.14, RMSE 0.54, 94 % of cells are within one level, and averaging within each quake first gives 0.48.

Calibration

  • The +0.76 offset is the median of (CDI − model MMI) over the 2,088 cells of the 29 older quakes: 0.765, stored as 0.76. The 28 scored quakes were excluded and the offset was not changed after scoring. Worden et al. (2012) was itself fitted to DYFI, so the offset corrects the whole chain rather than converting instrumental to reported intensity; before it, the model underestimates DYFI by 0.63 levels.
  • Event correction: for each quake, the mean over our stations of log10(observed / predicted PGV), clipped to ±0.5 and added to the prediction everywhere, like ShakeMap’s bias correction.

With the event correction: offset 0.43

  • With the event correction the offset is {{OFF_T}} (D), fitted the same way on the 29 older quakes by validate_mmi.py: MAE 0.42, bias +0.06 on the newer quakes. The correction already removes part of the underestimate, so 0.76 on top of it (D′) would overestimate by about 0.4 levels and is 0.14 worse in MAE, with both intervals excluding zero.
  • Confirmed quakes carry the correction and use {{OFF_T}}, in the server, the app and Android alike. Provisional pushes go out before sizing, carry no correction, and use 0.76 (C).

What the event correction is for

  • Alone it lowers the MAE by 0.18; once the offset is refitted it adds nothing against DYFI (D − C = +0.00), because most of the DYFI error is cell-to-cell scatter within a quake, which one number per quake cannot remove.
  • Its value is that the b·M term cancels, so our own magnitude error drops out: shifting M by +0.3 / −0.3 moves the estimate by 0.001 / 0.07 levels with the correction, against 0.42 / 0.37 without.
  • A single constant offset compresses the scale: cells with CDI 2–2.9 are overestimated by +0.20 and cells with CDI 5 or more underestimated by −0.46.

Implementation

The estimate is built in four steps: (1) the ground-motion formula predicts PGV, how fast the ground will move, from the magnitude, the distance and the ground type; (2) the Worden conversion turns that PGV into an intensity number; (3) the offset is added (see Calibration); (4) the result is rounded to a level from I to X.

Very close to the quake

  • How distance is measured: along the ground from the epicentre, the point on the surface directly above the quake, and never less than 1 km. The quake’s depth is not used.
  • Why that matters: a typical quake starts about 6–7 km down, so even standing on the epicentre you are about that far from it. The formula treats you as 1 km away instead, and predicts about 4 times the shaking it predicts at 5 km and about 8 times that at 10 km. Right above a quake its numbers are therefore untested and could be too high.
  • What the data shows: we have no test recordings within 5 km. From 5 to 20 km our stations recorded about twice what the formula predicts; beyond 20 km it is close (Table 3).
  • The usual fix: measure the distance to the quake itself, √(distance2 + depth2), and refit.

Measuring PGV

  • What PGV is: peak ground velocity, the fastest the ground moved during the quake.
  • How we get it from a station: take the station’s raw recording, convert it to true ground speed using the instrument’s known response, remove very fast vibrations above 18 Hz, keep the 30 s from just before the P wave, and take the largest value. The same code does this for the training data and for live quakes.
  • Which direction: each station records up-down, north-south and east-west, and we take the single largest value of the three. That is almost always a sideways direction: up-down is largest in only 7 % of recordings, and then only by about 2 %.
  • Units: the formula works in metres per second; the Worden conversion needs centimetres per second, so the value is multiplied by 100.

Turning PGV into an intensity level

PGV up to 3.4 cm/s:  MMI = 3.78 + 1.47·log10(PGV)     above 3.4 cm/s:  MMI = 2.89 + 3.16·log10(PGV)
  • What it is: Worden et al. (2012), the same conversion USGS ShakeMap uses: a gentle straight line for weak shaking and a steeper one for strong shaking. Before the offset, 0.1 cm/s gives 2.3, 1 cm/s gives 3.8 and 10 cm/s gives 6.05. The result is capped between 1 and 10, so 0.01 cm/s (which would give 0.8) becomes 1.
  • The weakest shaking: about half of the DYFI test areas are below level 2 before the offset. ShakeMap adds a third line for levels 1–2; we tried it and it matched people’s reports worse (error 0.47 vs 0.42, bias +0.22 vs +0.13), so we kept the two published lines.
  • Small bug: the server applies the 1–10 cap before adding the offset and the app after, so for the very weakest shaking the server says level II and the app level I. Both are shown as “Not felt”, so nothing visible changes, but the two should match.
