How the Detect model was trained

Trained on labelled 30 s single-station windows, not continuous data: 34,377 with a P arrival (8,238 quakes, M 2.0–7.2) and 16,366 without (random quiet times, plus the old live system’s false alarms), half and half in each batch.   • Quake windows: M 3+ quakes within 200 km of a station, plus a random 4,000 M 2–3 quakes within 100 km. One window per station where the live P picker finds the P wave (signal-to-noise ≥ 3), vertical component, stored as P − 26 s to P + 30 s. Each training example is a random 30 s crop with P anywhere 1–25 s in, because live data slides past the detector.
  • Random-time noise: 900 random times in 2000–2026 (any hour), 45 s at every station, kept only if no catalogued M 1+ quake is within 150 km and 150 s and no M 3+ within 500 km and 300 s. 896 times had data (median 17 stations each). Training uses random 30 s crops.
  • False alarms (hard negatives): windows where the first live daemon declared a quake that matches nothing in the catalogue. They teach the model what our own stations’ local noise looks like.
  • Input: demean, causal 1 Hz high-pass, unit standard deviation; the same function in training and live.
  • Training: each batch has 128 quake windows and 128 quake-free ones (a third of them false alarms), random polarity flips, binary cross-entropy, Adam, 12 epochs. Five seeds; the one with the best validation AUC is kept, and its threshold is set on validation.
  • Kept apart: all windows of one quake share its origin time, so a quake is never split between sets (one random-time noise snapshot at the validation/test boundary is).

Results, and the test on mixed data

On the 7,303 test windows: AUC 0.9998 (95 % CI 0.9997–0.9999), MCC 0.886, against 0.816 AUC for STA/LTA. That score uses the separate labelled windows. The realistic test is the replay: 80 held-out days (April 2022 – August 2026) of continuous archive data run through the full live pipeline (detect, pick, locate, size), quake and quiet periods together. It sent 145 pushes, all for real quakes; confirmed-event precision was 86 % on busy days and 79 % on random days (chance 4 % and 0 %), and it caught 68 % of M 3+ quakes.

Data validation

How the Size model was trained

Trained on all M 3+ quakes in the region from 2000 to Aug 2026 with at least 3 of our stations within 200 km, plus a random 4,000 M 2–3 quakes, using only stations within 100 km (6,243 quakes). For each quake it sees 30 s of shaking at every station that recorded it (each recording’s shape, how big it was, and the station’s distance) and learns to output the catalogue magnitude; in training the location, timing and stations are randomly disturbed to mimic live conditions. Both models are split 70/15/15 by time and by quake; no quake used to train one is in the other’s test set.

How it works, in plain terms

  • Which quakes: all M 3+ quakes in the region from 2000 to Aug 2026 with at least 3 of our stations within 200 km (thinned so that one aftershock swarm cannot dominate), plus a random 4,000 M 2–3 quakes, using only stations within 100 km. The small ones are there so the small quakes the live system detects are sized correctly. A quake is kept if at least 3 stations have a usable recording: 6,243 quakes, a median of 5 stations each.
  • What it sees: for each quake, 30 s of shaking at every station that recorded it, starting just before the P wave. Each recording is given as three things: its shape, how big it was, and how far the station is from the quake.
  • What it does: it reads each station’s recording, lets nearby stations compare notes, then combines all of them into one magnitude.
  • How it learns: it guesses the magnitude of each training quake, is told the catalogue answer, and adjusts itself to make the miss smaller, over 40 passes through the 4,370 training quakes. Five copies are trained this way and their answers are averaged.
  • Practice for live conditions: during training the quake’s location is moved by a few km, the timing is nudged by up to half a second, and some stations are left out, because the live system works from its own location estimate and whichever stations report first.
  • Split: 4,370 training quakes (2000 – July 2019), 936 validation (to Aug 2021) and 937 test quakes (Aug 2021 – Aug 2026). The PGV equation (next page) is fitted on the same 4,370 training quakes.

Technical details. Windows P − 5 s to P + 25 s, 3 components, aligned on the live P picker; each scaled to unit peak, with standardized log10 peak velocity and log10 distance as node features, plus four network amplitude/distance statistics. A CNN per station, two graph-convolution layers over the station graph (Gaussian weights, σ 50 km, cut at 150 km), one Transformer layer, a mean over stations and a small dense head. Mean squared error, Adam (learning rate 0.001, cosine decay, weight decay 0.0001), 40 epochs, 32 quakes per batch, five seeds averaged. Augmentation: epicentre moved by a normal error of 8 km in each direction, windows shifted by up to ±0.5 s, and either the nearest k ≥ 3 stations kept or 20 % dropped at random.

Results

On the 937 test quakes: R2 0.951 (95 % CI 0.943–0.959), MAE 0.10 magnitude units, against 0.886 for an amplitude-plus-distance regression. In a live-like test (10 km location error, only the nearest 3–6 stations) R2 is 0.939. The test quakes are all M 2.0–5.2 and only 29 training quakes are M 5 or larger, so large quakes are barely tested.

The two models use the same quakes

6,043 quakes are in both data sets (Detect also has quakes with fewer than 3 usable stations). Because each set is cut at its own 70 % and 85 % points, the cut dates differ slightly, but every shared Detect training quake is in the Size training split and every shared Detect test quake is in the Size test split. The only crossover is through validation: 219 Detect validation quakes are in the Size training (115) or test (104) split, and validation only picks seeds and thresholds.

Data validation

The ground-motion equation

Fitted on the Size model’s 4,370 training quakes (2000 to July 2019, 25,526 station records). Its 937 test quakes are from 2021–2026.

The estimate has two steps. A ground-motion equation predicts peak ground velocity (PGV) at a place, and Worden et al. (2012) converts PGV to MMI:

log10 PGV [m/s] = a + b·M + c·log10 R + d·R + e·log10(VS30 / 631)  (+ event correction)

with a = −5.697, b = 0.981, c = −0.868, d = −0.00456, e = −0.406. R is the epicentral distance in km (at least 1), VS30 comes from the USGS slope-based map, and 631 m/s is the median at our stations. The coefficients are a least-squares fit to log10 PGV of every training record (scripts/calibrate_shaking.py).

The records are the Size model’s (page 2): our 19 broadband stations (SCEDC archive), USGS-catalogue quakes of M 2.0 and above, stations within 200 km, at least 3 stations per quake. The coefficients use only the 4,370 training quakes; the PGV here is the largest absolute value of each station window (page 12). The test quakes all come after the training quakes, and every record of a quake is in the same split.

How well it fits held-out records

On the 6,197 test records the scatter of log10(observed / predicted PGV) is 0.33, a factor of about 2.1. After the event correction (page 8) it is 0.27. The residual by distance is on page 11.

Calibration

The +0.76 offset

The median of (DYFI − model) over the 29 older quakes. The 28 test quakes were excluded, and the offset was not changed after scoring.
offset = median over the 2,088 cells of the 29 older quakes of (CDI − MMImodel)

MMImodel is the Worden conversion of the equation’s PGV with the site term, without event correction and without offset. The result is 0.765, stored as 0.76. Fitted the same way with each quake’s event correction applied, the same fit gives 0.43, the offset used in that case (page 9). Each is one constant, with no dependence on magnitude, distance or intensity (scripts/validate_mmi.py).

What it corrects

The Worden et al. (2012) relation was itself derived from about 200,000 DYFI intensities matched to California ground-motion records (M 3.0–7.3, under 1 to about 400 km). So the offset is not a conversion from instrumental to reported intensity; it corrects the whole chain against DYFI. Before the offset the model underestimates DYFI by 0.63 levels, which on the lower Worden segment equals a factor of about 2.7 in PGV. Possible causes, not tested yet:

  • Our stations sit on stiffer ground than where people live (station median VS30 631 m/s), and the slope-based VS30 map captures only part of that.
  • The equation is dominated by M 3–4 records at 50–200 km, while the DYFI quakes are M 4–5.
  • DYFI cells average over 10 km, while Worden matched responses within 2 km of each station.

Calibration

What the event correction is for

Once the offset is refitted, the event correction does not improve the match to DYFI (C vs D). Its value is that it cancels our own magnitude error: a 0.3 error in M moves the estimate by under 0.1 level with it, about 0.4 without.

Why it cancels magnitude error

The correction is t = mean over stations of [log10 PGVobs − (a + bM + c log10R + dR + site)]. Adding t to the prediction at any place gives the mean observed log10 PGV plus the distance and site differences between that place and the stations. The b·M term drops out, so an error in our magnitude does not change the estimate, unless t reaches its ±0.5 clip.

Implementation

Very close to the quake

The formula measures distance along the ground from the epicentre (the point on the surface above the quake) and never lets it drop below 1 km. It ignores depth, although a typical quake starts about 6–7 km down, so right above a quake its prediction keeps climbing even though the quake is still several km below you. We have almost no recordings within 5 km to check this, and at 5–20 km the formula predicts only about half of what our stations recorded. The usual fix is to count the depth in the distance.
  • R = max(Repi, 1 km). There is no hypocentral or rupture distance, no fictitious depth and no magnitude saturation. Catalogue depths have a median of 6.5 km.
  • The prediction keeps rising as R−0.87 down to 1 km: ×4.2 between 5 and 1 km, ×8.1 between 10 and 1 km. Physically, within about one focal depth of the epicentre the distance to the source hardly changes.
  • Data: 7 training records within 5 km and 41 at 5–10 km; no test record within 5 km. Against DYFI, the 31 cells within 10 km show a bias of −0.05 to +0.18 (Table 14), but that is too few to judge.
  • For large magnitudes close in, the equation is outside its calibrated range (Table 15): only 29 training quakes are M 5 or larger, and the DYFI check reaches M 5.2.

Implementation

PGV and its units

PGV (peak ground velocity) is the fastest the ground moved. Each station records three directions (up-down, north-south, east-west), and we take the single largest value of the three, which is almost always a sideways one (up-down is largest in only 7 % of recordings). The formula works in metres per second; the Worden conversion needs centimetres per second, so the value is multiplied by 100.

The same code processes the training data and the live stream:

  1. Raw counts from the SCEDC archive (training) or the public SeedLink relay (live); HH channels at 100 Hz (the pre-2010 archive is BH at 40 Hz).
  2. Mean removed; instrument response removed to ground velocity (ObsPy, water level 60 dB), with no pre-filter or high-pass; resampled to 100 Hz where needed.
  3. Causal 4th-order Butterworth low-pass at 18 Hz, a common band for the 40 Hz and 100 Hz sources.
  4. A window around the P arrival: P − 6 s to P + 26 s in training, P − 5 s to P + 25 s live.
  5. PGV = the largest absolute value over all three components and the whole window.
  • Component: the peak over three components, including the vertical. It is not the geometric mean of the horizontals and not the vector sum. The vertical was the largest in 7.3 % of records and raises PGV by 0.007 log10 on average (1.7 %, about 0.01 MMI), so in practice this is the larger horizontal peak. Worden’s own component definition is still to be confirmed in the paper.
  • Units: m/s in the equation; multiplied by 100 to cm/s for the Worden conversion.
  • Live vs training window: PGV is identical in 93.2 % of training records. At 150 km and beyond it is 0.027 log10 (6 %) lower on average in the live window, because that window ends 1 s earlier and can cut the late S wave. Small, but it is a difference between training and live input.
Implementation

Weak shaking

Worden’s conversion from PGV to intensity is two straight lines, a gentle one for weak shaking (up to 3.4 cm/s) and a steeper one above, and the result is capped between I and X. USGS ShakeMap adds a third line for the very weakest shaking; we tried it and it matched people’s reports worse (error 0.47 vs 0.42), so we left it out. Small bug: the server and the app apply the cap in a different order, so for the very weakest shaking one shows level II and the other level I; both read “Not felt”.

The clamping bug

The Python code (server push wording, validation) clamps to 1 before adding the offset, so its lowest output is 1.76 (level II). The app and the Android code add the offset first, so their lowest is 1.0 (level I). A test over 315 cases found differences only where the model MMI is at the floor (109 cases). There is no visible effect today, because levels I and II share the label “Not felt”, but the code should be aligned.
